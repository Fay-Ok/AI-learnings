Energy

Power from space, and the nuclear backup plan

AI needs more electricity than the grid can add quickly. Elon Musk and Jeff Bezos both say the answer is in orbit, where the sun never sets. Here is what each of them actually said, what is really being tested, how much power space could bring, and why nuclear is the nearer-term bet.

The power problem

A single large AI campus can draw hundreds of megawatts to multiple gigawatts. One project in Cheyenne, Wyoming has grown to 2.7 gigawatts, nearly triple the state’s current electricity use. Globally, data centers are on track to use roughly 945 terawatt-hours a year by 2030, according to the IEA.

Today that power mostly comes from the grid, plus new gas plants built for the purpose. Entergy is building ten of them for Meta’s Hyperion campus in Louisiana. The full picture of what a campus consumes is in What it takes to keep AI running.

Space solar: the idea

Above the clouds, a solar panel in the right orbit gets sunlight almost all the time. Google’s researchers estimate it can be up to 8 times more productive than the same panel on the ground.

There are two ways to use that. The old idea, from the 1960s onward, is to beam the power down: collect sunlight on a huge satellite, turn it into microwaves or a laser, and catch it with a big antenna on the ground. The new idea is to skip the beam: put the AI chips in orbit next to the panels, and send only the answers down. That second idea is the one Musk and Bezos are talking about.

Musk’s version

On February 2, 2026, SpaceX confirmed it had acquired xAI, Musk’s AI company. Around the same time, SpaceX asked the U.S. FCC for permission to launch up to 1 million satellites for orbital data centers. Its math: 1 million tonnes of satellites a year, at 100 kilowatts of computing per tonne, adds 100 gigawatts of AI computing a year.

“The sun always shines in space!” Musk said, adding that “ultimately, there is a roadmap to launch a terawatt” a year. At Davos in January 2026 he went further: “the lowest cost place to put AI will be space and that will be true within two to three years, three at the latest.”

SpaceX’s own IPO filing is more careful. It says the orbital AI plans involve “unproven technologies” and “may not achieve commercial viability.” Note what Musk is not proposing: beaming power to the ground. His plan uses the sunlight in orbit and moves the computing to it.

SpaceX (SPCX) — launch + orbital compute

Bezos’s version

Bezos has pitched this for years. In 2019 he said, “We’ll move all heavy industry into space,” leaving Earth zoned for homes and light industry.

At Italian Tech Week in October 2025 he applied it to AI: “we’re going to start building these giant gigawatt data centers in space.” His reason: “These giant training clusters, those will be better built in space, because we have solar power there, 24/7.” His timeline was “10+ years,” and he bet on “not more than 20.”

In March 2026 his rocket company, Blue Origin, filed with the FCC for Project Sunrise: about 51,600 data center satellites in sun-synchronous orbits, launched on its New Glenn rocket. Like Musk, Bezos is talking about using the power in orbit, not sending it down.

Others actually testing it

Beaming power down. Caltech’s SSPD-1 launched in January 2023 and became the first to beam power wirelessly in space and point a detectable beam at Earth, onto a rooftop in Pasadena. It was funded by more than $100 million from Donald and Brigitte Bren. Japan’s OHISAMA demo is a 180 kg satellite with a 2 m² panel, meant to send about 1 kilowatt to the ground. UK start-up Space Solar has a letter of intent to deliver 30 megawatts to Reykjavik Energy in Iceland by 2030. Aetherflux, founded by Robinhood co-founder Baiju Bhatt, plans a roughly 1 kilowatt laser demo satellite in 2026.

China’s plans are the biggest on paper. Rocket scientist Long Lehao has described a kilometer-wide solar station in geostationary orbit, built with the coming Long March-9 rocket; one outside analyst expects a roughly 1 megawatt demo around 2030. Europe looked hard and paused: ESA’s SOLARIS study judged the technology “still relatively immature” for a demo mission.

Computing in orbit. Starcloud launched a 60 kg satellite carrying one Nvidia H100 GPU in November 2025, and has pitched a 5 gigawatt orbital data center. Google’s Project Suncatcher plans two prototype satellites with Planet by early 2027. Aetherflux has joined in too, with an orbital data center target of early 2027.

How much power, really?

Here is the honest gap. Today’s demo satellites move about 1 kilowatt. The 2.7 gigawatt Cheyenne campus would need about 2.7 million of them (my arithmetic). Space Solar’s 30 megawatt pilot, if it arrives in 2030, would cover about 1% of that campus.

NASA studied full-size versions in 2024. Its two reference designs each deliver about 2 gigawatts to the grid, roughly one campus, with a ground receiver up to 6 km wide, assuming operation from 2050. The cost came out at $0.61 to $1.59 per kilowatt-hour, versus $0.02 to $0.05 for wind and solar on the ground: 12 to 80 times more expensive. Launch was over 70% of the cost, at an assumed $1,000 per kg.

Computing in orbit avoids the beam, but it has its own math. Google says launch prices need to fall below about $200 per kg, which it expects around the mid-2030s, before orbital AI roughly matches ground costs. SpaceX’s 100 gigawatt-a-year plan needs 1 million tonnes of satellites a year; the whole world launched about 3,000 tonnes in 2025, more than 300 times less (my arithmetic). And heat is hard to get rid of in a vacuum: by one estimate, radiating away 1 megawatt takes about 1,200 m² of radiator, about four tennis courts.

So space power is real physics with real demos, but this decade it is kilowatts, not gigawatts.

Nuclear: the nearer-term bet

Nuclear runs around the clock, which is exactly what an AI campus wants, and the big tech companies are already signing deals. The fastest power comes from reactors that already exist.

Restarts and existing plants. Microsoft signed a 20-year deal in 2024 to restart Three Mile Island Unit 1, renamed the Crane Clean Energy Center: 835 MW, which Constellation still expects to start in 2027 (one grid analysis suggests a connection could slip to 2031). Amazon agreed in June 2025 to buy up to 1,920 MW from Talen’s Susquehanna plant through 2042.

New small reactors, mostly in the 2030s. Amazon and X-energy aim for more than 5 GW by 2039, starting with four reactors (320 MW) in Washington state, expandable to 960 MW. Google and Kairos Power aim for 500 MW by 2035; the first 50 MW plant in Tennessee is due in 2030. In January 2026 Meta announced deals for up to 6.6 GW by 2035, with Vistra (433 MW of upgrades to existing plants), Oklo (up to 1.2 GW in Ohio, as early as 2030) and TerraPower (up to eight Natrium reactors).

The numbers are the same order as an AI campus, which is why tech companies like nuclear. The catch is time: apart from restarts and upgrades, most of this lands in the 2030s.

Fusion: the long shot

Fusion is the bet with the biggest payoff and the least proof. In 2023 Microsoft agreed to buy power from Helion, which broke ground in July 2025 on a 50 MW plant in Malaga, Washington, targeted for 2028; a Helion executive has said full output might not come until 2030, and some physicists doubt the approach. In June 2025 Google agreed to buy 200 MW from Commonwealth Fusion Systems’ first ARC plant in Virginia, planned for the early 2030s. CFS is still building SPARC, the test machine meant to show its design can make more energy than it uses.

Treat both as unproven until a plant actually sends power to the grid.

What to watch

Now to 2027: whether Aetherflux and Japan’s OHISAMA actually beam power down; Google’s two Suncatcher prototypes; Starship launch prices; the Crane restart.

2028 to 2030: Helion’s first fusion power; Kairos’s 50 MW plant; Oklo in Ohio; Space Solar’s 30 MW pilot.

2030s: SMR fleets from Amazon, Google and Meta; launch costs near $200 per kg, the point where orbital AI starts to make sense on paper.
